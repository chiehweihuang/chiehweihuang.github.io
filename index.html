Work with me

The models change monthly. Physics does not. Motors still overheat, sensors still drift, supply chains still slip, and a robot that cannot survive manufacturing never reaches the field, however smart its policy. I spent seventeen years in that layer: mass production at Pegatron, six months of it embedded on a US customer's site in Austin, Texas, then Japan's robotics industry from 2018 to 2024, putting teleoperated robots into real stores and tactile sensors into real grippers. That is the layer where Physical AI claims live or die.

I write for the people who sign: investors running technical diligence on a robotics deal, teams about to pilot or buy a Physical AI product, whether it walks, rolls, or flies, and hardware companies building between Japan and Taiwan. If the analysis above is the kind of judgment you need on your own decision, this is how to buy it.

Deployment feasibility memo

For investors and buying teams. Every vendor says ready now, and most of what you know comes from the demo they built to sell you.

You send the product materials and describe the deployment target; we talk. You get a written memo: what the product claims, the conditions that make the demo work, the most likely failure points in the field, what to test first, and the evidence to require before the next commitment.

Hardware plan review

For hardware founders and the investors underwriting them. The plan says mass production within a year. Hardware schedules fail in predictable places, and they are cheapest to find before tooling starts.

You send the schedule, BOM, and production plan. You get written findings on whether the schedule, production path, supply chain, and engineering assumptions hold, ranked by risk, with the questions worth putting to vendors before signing anything.

Taiwan–Japan development risk review

For Japanese companies building with Taiwanese ODM/OEM partners, and Taiwanese teams serving Japanese clients. Both sides say everything is fine. Between two languages and two engineering cultures, something often gets lost.

You get a review of requirements, expectations, and execution risk across both sides, read in both languages: where the plan and the partner's reality diverge, and what to fix before it becomes a schedule slip.

The decisions these memos feed are large: tooling that would have to be recut, a pilot that burns two quarters, a check into a robot that cannot leave the lab. Against any of those, a fixed-scope written evaluation is a rounding error. And if I read your situation and do not believe I can improve the decision, I will say so before you pay.

Every engagement is fixed-scope with a written deliverable, and most of the work is remote by nature: documents, calls, and memos. When it needs to go deep into circuit design, control, or production execution, I bring in domain specialists and manage the review.

Track record

Tokyo robotics, prototype to production (2018–2024)

  • A haptic force-feedback exoskeleton (exiii, my first role in Japan): brought in to ready it for mass production and improved its board design; the productization was shelved when the VR market proved too small. Watching a sound prototype die for market reasons is also part of the education.
  • Teleoperated shelf-restocking robots deployed across 300 Japanese convenience stores, for a Bloomberg-covered Tokyo robotics company: motor-driver, power-distribution, brake, and gripper electronics; CAN safety architecture; field-failure investigation; an NVIDIA Jetson carrier-board program; and PCBA production management into 2024.
  • Flexible-PCB (FPC) design for magnetic tactile skin sensors on robot grippers at a Tokyo tactile-sensing company, and consumer health-device electronics with project-schedule control for a Tokyo hardware startup.

The recurring job was the same: judge which prototype was ready to advance, which process would survive scale, and why the thing that worked in the demo failed in the field.

Pegatron: notebooks and tablets to mass production (2009–2016)

Seven years at one of Taiwan's largest ODMs, from electrical engineer to manager of a four-engineer team: schematic and layout design, engineering-change review, new-product introduction from prototype into mass production, and BOM, schedule, tooling, and supplier-plan reviews with the factory. Included six months embedded on a US customer's site in Austin, Texas, coordinating design, validation, and factory teams across borders. This is where the Taiwan-side manufacturing judgment comes from.

AI-assisted practice (2024–present)

I work AI-assisted by default: research, claim cross-checking, and the writing behind every memo. I also build my own evaluation infrastructure, currently a procurement decision pipeline that enforces official-source verification for every price and spec it compares, data pipelines connecting ECAD/MCAD design data to sourcing and BOM decisions, and an accessibility inspection tool that scores interfaces against WCAG 2.2, the same discipline behind this site's own accessibility statement. Same judgment, delivered faster, with evidence attached.

Since April 2024 I also serve as a founding board member of the APAC Authentic Inclusion Association, researching how AI and emerging technology can serve elders and people with disabilities.
